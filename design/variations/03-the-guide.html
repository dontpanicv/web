Entry 0x01Life formRating: mostly harmless

Kurka, Vojtěch (“V”)

Carbon-based. Found in Brno, Czechia. Answers to a single letter, which saves everybody time.

Head of R&D and co-founder at Meiro, which is to say he is paid to be curious and, on better days, to be right. Field researchers describe him as a person who likes knowing how things work, a condition for which there is no known cure and for which he has never sought one.

IN HIS OWN WORDSI like knowing how things work — from CPUs and protocols to databases and containers. Almost ten years of building a data platform in production taught me most of it. Now I spend that curiosity on AI: local models, agentic workflows, and getting it all into production for real customers. If I can't explain it to someone else, I don't understand it yet.

Some of what he knows was picked up out of curiosity; the rest, by his own account, by debugging production. He is also the person who ends up explaining everything, on stage when the occasion calls for it. He maintains that teaching is how he verifies he actually understands something, which is exactly what someone who enjoys explaining things would say.

His working domains are customer data infrastructure, identity resolution and real-time pipelines. His current enthusiasms are running Qwen locally, agentic workflows and self-learning data analysis tools, all of them, he insists, “in production, not just demos”. His own one-line summary reads: “I write code no one wants to read, but everyone needs to have. Data whatever.”

Entry 0x02Day job · since 2018Rating: load-bearing

Meiro

A company in which customer data goes in at one end and comes out the other knowing who it belongs to.

Meiro makes customer data infrastructure, known in the trade as a CDP. It comes in two halves. Pipes does the plumbing: sources, events, identity and destinations. Engage does something useful with the result: audiences, journeys and campaigns.

V co-founded it in 2018, ran it as CTO until 2024 and has since been Head of R&D, a title that lets him take things apart with a clear conscience. He owned the product architecture, the development and the implementations, including the edge cases that only appear once real customer data arrives.

The newest resident is the AI Analyst, an analytical agent built on bash, a mounted in-memory filesystem and a JSON renderer instead of the usual tool-calling loop (see Tool-Calling Loop, Beyond the). The whole thing can be self-hosted: “AI included, that you own, control and audit.”

Entry 0x03Swift · ★ 41Rating: mostly harmless*

Mycroft

*Unless you ask it to rewrite itself, which it will.

A self-modifying, durable agent that lives on your iPhone. Brain on the phone, hands on your machines. It is named after Mycroft Holmes, the brother who stayed in his armchair while other people did the legwork, and it has adopted the working style completely.

The agent loop, its state and its own source code all live on the phone. It edits its own behaviour and its own screen, keeps the known-good generations, and can roll back when an idea turns out to be the other kind. The legwork is done by hands, a small daemon for macOS and Linux that gives it files, a shell and supervised jobs which carry on when the phone wanders off.

It also drives a Mac, takes voice in every thread, and keeps one infinite chat whose older memories fold themselves into a tree of summaries. V describes it as “my first creation on iOS”, and then, accurately, as “a mouthful”.

Entry 0x04TypeScriptRating: domesticated

Golem

A family AI assistant and second brain. No cloud, no central server, no clay.

FROM THE READMEIn Prague legend, Rabbi Loew shaped the Golem from clay to serve and protect his community. This one is made from SQLite and Markdown.

One server at home, one app for the whole family on the web, the iPhone and Android. Everybody talks to the same Golem, and it knows who is asking. It remembers: each topic keeps a brief, and a learning loop keeps short facts about each person and how things are done in that particular household.

It also does real work on the family's own machines, which is where most assistants politely stop. Being a well-raised Golem, it asks before it sends, buys or deletes anything. The original, the legend records, was less careful.

Entry 0x05TypeScriptRating: quiet

Shadow

A shadow company for your team. Considerably less sinister than it sounds.

Every employee gets a shadow: an agent that reads their Slack and their mail, keeps track of what each conversation is actually asking of them, and helps do the work when asked. Not before. The Guide regards this restraint as the single most advanced feature in the product.

It lives in Slack and stays quiet, speaking only when you must act or when you asked it to. It runs on your own server, on one SQLite database and a pile of Markdown files, so the company's conversations stay where the company left them.

Entry 0x06Article · 2026-04-29Rating: recommended reading

Tool-Calling Loop, Beyond the

How We Built an AI Analyst with Bash, a Mounted Filesystem, and a JSON Renderer.

The Guide's summary: an agent was observed drowning in fifty thousand rows of its own JSON and was rescued by being handed a shell. The author's summary is longer, more useful and begins below.

▚ THE TEXT, AS FILED

Most AI features built today follow the same recipe: take the Vercel AI SDK, register a handful of tools, write a system prompt, and stream the result into a markdown component. It works. It is also where most teams stop — and where most AI products start to feel limited.

This works great for operational assistants: “create an audience”, “show me the last failed delivery”, “explain what this attribute does”. The conversation is short, each tool returns a small, well-shaped object, and the markdown is the answer. It stops working the moment the model needs to think with data.

The first wall is large tool outputs. Whatever a tool returns goes straight into the model's context. A query that returns 50k rows is now 50k rows of JSON sitting in the conversation, on every subsequent turn, paid for in tokens, and pushing earlier reasoning out of the window. Tool results are a terrible place to put data.

The second wall is subtler and arrives earlier: metadata bloat. To answer almost any analytical question the model needs to know what exists, and the naive way to expose that is one inventory tool per kind of thing. The model, being agreeable, calls all of them at the start of every conversation “to orient itself”. It cannot search any of it. There is no grep.

So the Analyst gets one tool, bash, and a filesystem with the inventory already written to disk:

const workspace = new Bash();
await workspace.writeFile('/context/inventory.json', JSON.stringify(inventory));
await workspace.writeFile('/context/instructions.md', customInstructions);

Two more ideas complete it: a SQL execution layer that mounts query results as files instead of returning rows, and a JSON renderer with a predefined component manifest, so the answer arrives as charts and tables rather than a wall of prose.

Entry 0x07Article · 2026-02-10Rating: tidy

Code, Where It Goes

Stop Letting AI Agents Guess Where Code Goes.

An AI agent asked to add a feature will put the code somewhere. Whether that somewhere is correct depends less on the agent than on whether the codebase has an obvious answer. This article describes a modular-monolith refactor designed so that it does.

Three things do the work: a predictable module layout, so every module looks like every other module; centralized auth, so nobody reinvents it in a corner; and a strict public.ts boundary, the one door through which a module may be used, which stops architecture from quietly dissolving.
